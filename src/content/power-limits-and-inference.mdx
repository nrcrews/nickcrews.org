Inference has two phases, and they stress a GPU in opposite ways.

**Prefill** reads the prompt. Every weight pulled from memory is used for thousands of tokens at
once, so the cores are the bottleneck. **Decode** writes the answer, one token per request per
step. Every step reads every weight and does little math with it, so memory is the bottleneck and
the cores spend much of their time waiting.

A power cap has one lever: the core clock. So the two phases should react differently. I measured
how differently.

**Setup:** eight H100 SXM cards (700 W max) running vLLM, four serving Qwen3-8B and four serving
Qwen3-14B, each swept from 700 W down to 200 W with 32 requests in flight. Every card is compared
with its own uncapped run. The two models agree within a few points; the charts show the 8B. Code and
raw data: [github.com/nrcrews/tokens-per-watt](https://github.com/nrcrews/tokens-per-watt).

## Decode barely notices

At 415 W, 59% of the card's maximum, decode keeps 96% of its speed. Prefill keeps 74%.

![Speed against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/speed.png)

*Shaded: the limits where decode keeps at least 95% of its uncapped speed.*

## The clock explains it

At 415 W both phases lose about a quarter of their clock. Prefill loses the same quarter of its
speed: it is compute-bound, so speed follows clock one to one. Decode loses 4%. Its cores were
waiting on memory, and waiting at a lower clock costs almost nothing.

![Core clock against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/clock.png)

*Decode holds full clock down to 555 W. Uncapped, it only draws about 525 W, so the top of the
range does nothing for it.*

## Slower is cheaper

A chip's dynamic power scales with voltage squared times frequency. A lower clock lets the
voltage drop too, so each operation costs less energy, and energy per token falls faster than
speed.

At 415 W, decode gets 23% more tokens per joule and prefill 25%. No other limit gives both a
gain that large.

![Tokens per joule against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/efficiency.png)

*Decode's efficiency peaks lower, around 270–345 W, but by 270 W it has lost 29% of its speed.*

## Where it breaks

Below about 345 W the clock collapses. At 200 W decode runs at 345 MHz and keeps about half its
speed. Prefill hits the wall sooner: from 415 W to 345 W its clock falls by a third while the
limit falls only 17%.

The likely cause is the voltage floor. Once the voltage can't go lower, every watt saved has to
come from the clock alone, while memory and the rest of the board keep drawing their share. I
didn't measure voltage, so treat that as a hypothesis.

Batch size moves the line. More requests in flight means more math per weight read, so decode
starts to behave like prefill.

![Decode speed by requests in flight](/images/power-limits-and-inference/concurrency.png)

*At 270 W, decode keeps 85% of its speed with one request in flight and 71% with 32.*

## The PCIe card

The 350 W H100 PCIe shows the same split without the cliff. At 245 W decode keeps 96% of its
speed with 21% more tokens per joule, and prefill keeps 68%. At its 200 W floor decode still keeps
87%, with the clock near 1 GHz. One thing I can't explain yet: on PCIe, prefill gets slightly less
efficient under a cap.

## What to do with it

- **Decode doesn't need full power.** On an H100 SXM, 415 W costs 4% of decode speed for about a
  fifth less energy per token.
- **Prefill does.** Its speed tracks the clock.
- **Don't go below about 345 W on SXM.** The clock falls off a cliff and the savings go with it.
- **Batch size matters.** The bigger the batch, the more decode behaves like prefill.

These numbers count GPU power only, not CPU, networking or cooling. They cover two models, one
engine and batch sizes of 1 and 32. The raw data, every chart and the code to rerun it on your own
GPU are on GitHub: [nrcrews/tokens-per-watt](https://github.com/nrcrews/tokens-per-watt).
