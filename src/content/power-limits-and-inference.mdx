## What I measured

Every GPU has a power limit, and you can turn it down. I wanted to know what that actually costs
when the GPU is serving a model.

The catch is that inference isn't one job. It's two. **Prefill** reads your prompt, all at once.
**Decode** writes the response, one token at a time. They lean on the hardware in very different
ways, so I measured them separately. Lumping them together would be like timing a sprint and a
marathon and reporting the average.

## How

I rented eight H100 SXM GPUs (700 W max each) and ran vLLM on all of them: four serving Qwen3-8B,
four serving Qwen3-14B. Then I walked each GPU's power limit down from 700 W to 200 W. At every
step I hit it with prefill-only and decode-only traffic, 32 requests in flight, and read energy
straight off the GPU's own counter.

Each GPU is compared against its own uncapped run, so a slightly faster chip doesn't skew things.
The two models land within a few points of each other, so the charts show the 8B. Code and raw
data are on [GitHub](https://github.com/nrcrews/tokens-per-watt) if you want to check my math.

## Results

Decode barely noticed. At 415 W, about 60% of full power, it kept 96% of its speed. Prefill kept
74%.

![Speed against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/speed.png)

It gets better. At that same 415 W, decode got 23% more tokens out of every joule. Prefill got
25% more too. It just took longer to get them.

![Tokens per joule against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/efficiency.png)

There's a floor, though. Below about 345 W both phases fall off a cliff, and at 200 W decode is
down to half its speed. Batch size matters too: at 270 W, decode keeps 85% of its speed with one
request in flight but only 71% with 32.

![Decode speed by requests in flight](/images/power-limits-and-inference/concurrency.png)

I ran the same sweep on the smaller H100 PCIe (350 W max). Same story, no cliff: decode still
keeps 87% of its speed at 200 W.

## Why

A power limit really only has one lever. It turns down the core clock.

Prefill is a math problem. Every weight it reads from memory gets used for thousands of prompt
tokens, so the cores are busy the whole time. At 415 W the clock drops about 25%, and prefill
slows down by the same 25%. Fewer cycles, less math.

Decode is a memory problem. Every token means reading every weight from memory, but it only does a
little math with each one. The cores finish early and sit there waiting for the next batch of
weights to arrive. At 415 W its clock also drops about 25%, but its speed only drops 4%. Turns out
you can wait just as well at a lower clock.

![Core clock against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/clock.png)

The energy savings come from physics. A chip's power scales with frequency times voltage
squared, and a lower clock lets the voltage drop too. So energy falls faster than speed does.

Bigger batches give decode more math to do per weight it reads, which makes it act more like
prefill. That's why it feels the cap more at 32 requests than at one.

And the cliff? My guess is the voltage hits its minimum. After that, every watt saved has to come
out of the clock alone, which is how you end up at 345 MHz at 200 W. I didn't measure voltage, so
that part is a hunch, not a result.
