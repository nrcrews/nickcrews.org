## What I measured

How a GPU's power limit changes inference speed and energy use, measured separately for the two
phases of inference:

- **Prefill**: reading the prompt.
- **Decode**: writing the response, one token at a time.

## How

Eight H100 SXM GPUs (700 W max) running vLLM: four serving Qwen3-8B, four serving Qwen3-14B. I
stepped each GPU's power limit from 700 W down to 200 W and, at each step, measured prefill and
decode throughput with 32 requests in flight, along with energy from the GPU's own counter. Each
GPU is compared with its own uncapped run. The two models agree within a few points, so the charts
show the 8B. Code and raw data are on [GitHub](https://github.com/nrcrews/tokens-per-watt).

## Results

At 415 W, decode keeps 96% of its speed. Prefill keeps 74%.

![Speed against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/speed.png)

At the same 415 W, decode gets 23% more tokens per joule and prefill 25%.

![Tokens per joule against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/efficiency.png)

Below about 345 W, both fall off sharply. At 200 W decode keeps about half its speed. Larger
batches feel the cap sooner: at 270 W decode keeps 85% of its speed with one request in flight
and 71% with 32.

![Decode speed by requests in flight](/images/power-limits-and-inference/concurrency.png)

The 350 W H100 PCIe shows the same pattern without the sharp drop: at 245 W decode keeps 96% of
its speed, and at 200 W it still keeps 87%.

## Why

A power limit works by lowering the GPU's core clock.

Prefill does a lot of math for every weight it reads from memory, so it's limited by the cores.
At 415 W the clock falls about 25%, and prefill slows by the same amount.

Decode reads every weight from memory for each token but does little math with it, so it's
limited by memory. Its cores spend much of their time waiting. At 415 W its clock also falls about
25%, but its speed falls only 4%, because memory runs just as fast as before.

![Core clock against power limit](/images/power-limits-and-inference/clock.png)

Running at a lower clock is also more efficient. A chip's power scales with frequency times
voltage squared, and a lower clock allows a lower voltage, so energy per token falls faster than
speed.

Larger batches do more math per weight read, so decode behaves more like prefill and slows more.

Below about 345 W the clock collapses, to 345 MHz at 200 W. The likely cause is that the voltage
reaches its minimum, so further savings have to come from the clock alone. I didn't measure
voltage, so that part is a hypothesis.
